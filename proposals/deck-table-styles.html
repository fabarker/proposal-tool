Where we areWhy the slides look wrong today

The deck scales the workbook’s geometry by one factor: Excel column widths, and spreadsheet row heights — 17pt rows for 12.5pt text, a 37pt header. On a slide, that lands the tables narrow and small.

The sizing fix is the same in all ten versions: each table fills its box’s width, rows are sized to the text rather than to a spreadsheet, and the text is the largest that still fits. Version 1 is that fix and nothing else.

Your questionHow to find the best format

  1. Judge the real thing at the real size. Every table here comes out of sheetDoc’s builders with real figures and renderNumber’s text. Every slide is a true 16:9 canvas — 13.33 × 7.5 in, drawn at 1pt = 1px — scaled to your screen. Where Calibri and Aptos Narrow are installed (they come with Office) you see the real faces; elsewhere, close stand-ins.
  2. Choose a system, not ten tables. Each version is one set of rules applied to all four tables, so the deck stays consistent. Combining is fine: “3, with 7’s proposal column”.
  3. Measure the sizing, don’t eyeball it. Each table is shrunk until it fits its box without clipping a figure, and the point size it lands at is printed under the slide — green from 10pt, amber from 8.5pt, red below.
  4. Proof the finalists in PowerPoint. Browsers and PowerPoint measure text differently. PowerPoint is installed on this Mac, so once you’ve chosen one or two, I’ll render them as real .pptx and export them through PowerPoint itself before building anything.
  5. Then lock it in as the deck’s theme, with tests pinning it.

What this does to the 1:1 promise. Sizing never threatens it — a slide is not a sheet, so sizing is per medium by nature. Styling does. Versions 1 and 2 keep the workbook’s styling exactly; 7 adds one thing; the rest restyle. A restyle can go to both files (one engine — the workbook changes too), or to the deck only, which narrows the promise from “same styling” to “same content, same numbers”.

What splitting does and doesn’t buy. The allocation table is limited by its height, not its width: 32 rows, most of them asset lines. On a slide of its own it gets wider columns but not larger text — it fits at the same size either way. Only tighter rows move that number (version 2 reaches 10pt). The risk table, at 24 rows, fits at around 11pt in both configurations.

Tables that share a slide share a type size. On the combined slide, each table is fitted alone, then both step down to the smaller size, and the shorter table opens its rows to use the height it gave back. Both halves end at the bottom, and the slide reads as one piece rather than two.

One decision the combined slide forces. The risk sheet opens by repeating the allocation by category and the three metrics — the same figures as the left-hand table. Side by side, that shows everything twice. Dropping them from the risk table (the default here) takes it from 33 rows to 24, and that is what lets both halves sit at a readable size. Yours to callDecisions

  • Which version — a number, or a combination (“3 with 7’s proposal column”). My read: 1 fixes exactly what’s wrong today without touching the 1:1 promise; 7 is the smallest step beyond it, and makes the proposal the thing the eye lands on.
  • The repeated rows on the risk table — drop them (the default here) or keep them. The choice applies to both configurations, so the risk table reads the same wherever it appears.
  • For versions 3–10: both files, or the deck only? I’d apply a restyle to both. One engine was the point, and a workbook that looks different from its own presentation invites the question of which one is right.
  • The 46 / 54 split on the combined slide gives the risk table its extra columns. If the allocation labels feel cramped, 50 / 50 is one line to change.

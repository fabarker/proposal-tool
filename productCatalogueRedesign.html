Catalogue Redesign

The product catalogue, rethought for the people who will actually use it: investment professionals who already know the products, comparing candidates against a slot in a portfolio under time pressure. Three complete directions to compare side by side, one comparison of how the numbers should be drawn, and the decisions that separate them — built on the data that will actually exist.

2 September 2026Status: Option A built — 2 September 2026 (D63)Revision 2: no realised fund metrics. The data is the delivered sheet plus distribution yield and minimum investment; nothing else is assumed.

Who is using it, and what that changes

The catalogue today is an admin's inventory: a sortable table of the delivered extract, filters derived from its columns, a drawer saying which sleeves hold a product. Adequate for is this product here and where is it used. The brief describes a different user with a different question — which of these implements this slot best — and that reframes almost everything:

  • They start from a slot, not from a product. A PWA implementing Investment Grade Fixed Income for a US Onshore book already knows the category, the vehicles the book can hold, the liquidity the client needs and the currency. The catalogue's job is to get from that constraint set to a shortlist in seconds — so the organising axis is category first, then constraints, then the numbers, and the constraints should be one click each with counts, not a search-and-hope.
  • The numbers are few, and that is a gift. With no realised metrics, a product compares on cost, distribution yield, minimum investment and liquidity, against a set of categorical constraints (vehicle, style, source, exposure currency, fee group). Four numbers fit in anyone's eye at once. The catalogue does not need to become a screen of figures; it needs to make those four unmissable and everything else quiet.
  • They compare, they do not browse. A shortlist of two to five candidates seen side by side beats any amount of scrolling. That means a way to pin rows and a place where pinned rows line up.
  • They know the tickers. Search must be instant on ticker and name fragments, reachable from the keyboard (/), and it must never reset the filters — a search is a narrowing of a view, not a new view.
  • They come back. Repeat use rewards persistence: the view's state (filters, sort, columns, pins) belongs in the URL so it can be bookmarked and shared.
  • They are on a desktop, in a hurry. Density is a feature. Sticky column headers, a sticky first column when the table scrolls sideways, and running category headers turn scrolling into paging. Responsiveness means holding up at 1100px, not fitting a phone.

The data, stated plainly. Everything below is drawn from the eleven fields the catalogue already carries — id, name, ticker, asset class, style, vehicle, source, liquidity, exposure currency, product cost, fee group — plus the two the delivery will gain, distribution yield and minimum investment, plus two things the tool already knows and can join in: the management fee at the open mandate's tier and level (the fee card, D55) and which sleeves and books hold a product (the repository, D57). One figure is derived and marked as such wherever it appears: net yield, distribution yield less all-in cost, because it is the number a PWA is actually computing in their head. The yields and minimums in the mockups are invented; the names, costs and attributes are the real delivered ones.

What every option shares

Some choices are simply better for a professional decision-support table, and there is no point offering their opposites. Every option below is built on them:

No images

A manager's logo tells a same-house catalogue nothing, and a product has no picture. With no time series there is no sparkline to argue for either. The only graphic worth a row is a data bar behind a figure, and only where ranking a column by eye is the point.

Numbers as numbers

Right-aligned tabular figures in the condensed face; cost and yield to two decimals, minimum investment as compact money ($5m, $1k, —); a true minus sign; units in the header, once, never in the cell.

Liquidity is the risk signal

Without volatility, the one thing in the data that says be careful is liquidity. Daily is plain; Monthly and Quarterly are amber; Drawdown is red. It is the only place colour is used on a row attribute, so it cannot be missed.

Colour only where it means something

The accent is for selection and pins. Beyond liquidity, semantic colour marks two states: a product not yet placed in any sleeve, and a product whose minimum exceeds what the open mandate would put in it.

Sticky everything that orients

Column headers stay while you scroll down; the product column stays while you scroll across; category headers, where used, run along the top of their group.

Keyboard first, state in the URL

/ searches, ↑↓ move, space pins, enter opens, c compares. Filters, sort, columns and pins encode into the query string: a view is a link.

Three directions

Option A

The terminal

Maximum density, keyboard first, every field visible at once. A facet rail on the left carries every constraint with counts; the table is 34px rows with the four numbers on the right; pinned rows collect in a tray at the foot that opens into the comparison. With fewer numeric columns than the first revision, this is less of a wall than it was — the width now goes on the categorical attributes, which are the constraints an expert is scanning for.

Optimises for

  • Speed for expert repeat users — twenty products in view, every attribute in a column, no clicks to reveal.
  • Constraint-driven narrowing: the facet rail is the search for most sessions.
  • Cheapest to maintain: the delivered table plus two fields, nothing derived but net yield.

Costs

  • Intimidating on first contact; nothing explains itself.
  • Fourteen columns still want a column picker and a sticky first column, and at 1100px it scrolls sideways.
  • Looks like a terminal — a virtue to this audience, a vice to anyone who wants it to look like the rest of the tool.

Option B strongest

The ledger

Organised the way the work is: products grouped under running category headers in the allocation's own order, each group sorted by all-in cost, with a comparison panel on the right where pinned candidates line up field by field. The table is 40px rows; cost and yield carry a small data bar so either column can be ranked by eye; liquidity is a pill; a product whose minimum exceeds what the open mandate would put in it is dimmed and flagged. This revision also gives the comparison panel section headings, because with the numbers gone it is mostly terms, and terms want grouping.

Optimises for

  • The slot workflow: the category you are implementing is a heading you scroll to, and everything under it is a candidate.
  • Comparison as a first-class act: pin, and the panel fills; the lowest cost and highest yield are marked.
  • The constraint that bites: a minimum you cannot meet is shown before you have pinned the product, not after.

Costs

  • Grouping fights a global sort — sorting by yield sorts within each group, which is what you want for a slot and not for "highest yield anywhere". The Flat toggle is the answer.
  • The comparison panel takes 300px; at 1100px it becomes a bottom sheet.
  • Data bars need a scale per column, and a bar on cost means "more" while a bar on yield means "better" — two colours, and a key.

Option C

The sheet

A calmer lookup tool: a narrower table carrying only the instant tier at 46px rows, and a persistent panel on the right that shows one product in full — its terms, the fee ladder at every tier, where it is used and which books offer it. Comparison is two products at a time, by pinning one and selecting another. This looks most like the rest of the Proposal Tool. It is weaker than it was: with no description and no documents in the data, the panel is a short list of terms and a fee ladder, and a panel that short does not need to be persistent.

Optimises for

  • Reading one product properly, with the fee ladder answering "what would this cost at other sizes" in place.
  • Newer and occasional users, who get context without a column picker.
  • Visual continuity with the implementation table and the fee card.

Costs

  • Fewer rows per screen, and comparison across five candidates means five clicks and memory.
  • The panel has 380px and, on this data, about 300px of content. The rest is air that a comparison would use better.
  • Under time pressure, breathing room is scrolling.

How the numbers are drawn

Layout and data treatment are separate decisions — any of the three layouts can carry any of these. The same five products, four ways. With four numbers rather than nine the question is narrower, and mostly about whether the two that matter most — cost and yield — should be readable as a shape.

1 · Plain figures baseline

Right-aligned tabular numerals, two decimals, minimums as compact money. Nothing to maintain, nothing to misread. Ranking a column takes a sort.

Where this reasoning lands

B is the strongest direction, and the narrower data makes the case cleaner, not weaker. With four numbers rather than nine, the terminal's advantage — everything in view — costs it little to give up, and what it gives up is the two things that matter most to this workflow: products in the order the work happens in, and a comparison where the eye already is. The minimum-investment flag belongs to B alone; it is the one constraint that quietly disqualifies a product, and B is the only option that says so before the product is pinned. C's argument was its panel, and on this data the panel is a fee ladder and a list of terms — worth having, as B's drawer, on demand.

The data treatment to pair with it is 2 + 3 + 4: liquidity as the signal everywhere, bars on cost and yield in the table, and net yield as a column — the last one only once its definition is agreed.

What I would need from you before this is worth committing to

1
Who is the user, formally.

The brief describes PWAs; the current catalogue is admin-only behind requireAdmin. If PWAs are to use it, the gate becomes requireAuth, the entry point moves out of the admin bar and into the sleeve tier, and compliance signs off on showing yields to advisers. A one-line change in the service; a real change in what the page is.

2
What distribution yield is, exactly.

Trailing twelve months or indicated? Gross or net of the product's own cost? In the exposure currency? With an as-of date per product, or one for the whole delivery? The column header has to say what it is in three words, and "yield" alone is not enough for an adviser to rely on.

3
What minimum investment means for each vehicle.

Per account, presumably — but in which currency for a Local-exposure fund, and is an SMA's minimum the manager's or the platform's? And what the value is for a product with none: blank, zero, or a dash the reader should trust.

4
Whether to show net yield at all, and what cost it nets.

It is derived, it is the number people compute anyway, and it is the most useful column in the mockups. It is also the one most likely to be misread as a return. If shown: net of product cost alone (defined without a mandate) or of all-in (defined only with one)?

5
Whether to flag a minimum the mandate cannot meet, and against what.

The mockups compare the minimum with the notional the open mandate puts into the whole category — the most generous reading. A stricter one needs the sleeve chosen first. Either is a rule the desk should own.

6
Whether the two new fields ride the same extract.

Two more columns in products.csv is one reader and one delivery. A separate feed joined on ProductId is a second thing that can go stale and a second provenance line.

7
Whether shortlists and saved views persist, and for whom.

Per session is free (it is the URL). Per user needs a small store keyed by kerberos and a decision about private versus shared with the desk.

8
Overlay or page.

The catalogue lives inside the repository console today. A comparison workflow wants the whole viewport; that argues for it becoming its own view of the tool, reached from the sleeve tier. It changes the plumbing more than the design.

9
The default density and the default grouping

— best decided by putting the built thing in front of two or three of the people described in the brief for a week, with both toggles in the toolbar, and reading what they leave it set to.
